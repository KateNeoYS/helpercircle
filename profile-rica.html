The essentials

Four years of dementia care

She joined the family in 2021 to help look after their mother, who had developed dementia, alongside the household work. She stayed through the whole course of it, until the lady passed away in December 2024.

What that care involved

Dressing and showering at first, then toileting as the lady grew weaker, and finally full bedside care once she became bed-bound in 2024.

She had not done it before

This was her first time caring for someone with dementia, and there was no shared language — the lady spoke neither English nor any language Rica speaks. Her employer says she worked it out anyway.

How she handled distress

In her employer’s words, she never talked back when the lady got upset for no reason. She spoke in comforting tones and distracted her with stories until the mood passed.

Why that role ended

The lady she cared for passed away, and the family no longer needed a helper. Rica stayed on until her contract ended.

Six years before that, with children

From 2016 she was with a British Indian family, taking on a newborn and a two-year-old and staying until they were eight and ten. She moved to the household with the grandmother when that family relocated to London — the two households are related, so she stayed within the same family.

Currently

Since January 2026 with a British family and their baby, now eight months old. She cooks the baby’s food and Western meals for the household.

Why she is moving on

The mother is relocating to London with the baby and only the father will remain in Singapore, so the household no longer needs a live-in helper.

Ten years in Hong Kong

Before Singapore she worked ten years in Hong Kong. Her last family there brought her over. She speaks English and Cantonese.

Her employer’s recommendation

In her employer’s words, and ours.

Rica came to this household from a related family she had already worked for since 2016, to help care for their mother, who had developed dementia. She stayed through the whole course of the illness. Her employer wrote this letter in January 2026.

In her employer’s own words:

“Despite the language barrier and despite it being her first time caring for someone with dementia, Rica intuitively could figure out what mom wanted — often even better than me. When she could not, Rica would patiently coax mom for more information. When that did not work, Rica was able to defuse the situation and prevented mom’s meltdown by her calm and humble attitude.”

“Mom was a highly independent woman who’s very guarded and cautious, and dementia made her paranoid. Yet Rica’s love, compassion, and respect towards her won her over, and in only a month’s time mom was happily accepting Rica’s help with daily activities such as dressing, showering — and later even toileting when mom became much weaker.”

“Rica never talked back when mom got upset for no reason; instead, she addressed mom in comforting tones and distracted her with anecdotes, and soon mom was back to her good mood.”

“When mom’s condition worsened in 2024 and she became bed-bound, Rica patiently and tirelessly waited on mom to ensure her comfort.”

“Rica’s maturity, dependability, integrity and wealth of experience would make her an exceptional asset to any household.”

Where she’d thrive next: a family with an elderly parent, particularly one with dementia or heading that way. Very few helpers have seen it through from the early confusion to the final months, and fewer still are described this way by the family who watched them do it. She is equally at home with a baby, which is what she does now.

Her employer’s full letter is available to interested families, and she has offered to answer questions directly.
